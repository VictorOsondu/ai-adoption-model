# The Six Stages of AI Adoption

*A model built around identity, not tooling.*

Most frameworks for AI adoption measure what an organisation has: tools deployed, use cases live, data infrastructure, hours logged. Those models are useful if you're making a board-level case for investment. They give you language and a roadmap.

They can't tell you why a specific person — one who already has the tools, already sat the training, already had the moment where AI genuinely surprised them — still isn't taking the next step.

That gap isn't in the org chart. It's in the person. And the variable it turns on is identity: whether someone believes, underneath everything, that they're the kind of person who goes further.

This model sorts people by that internal experience rather than by their tooling. Most people can locate themselves before they finish reading, because the feeling lands before the description does.

---

## The stages at a glance

| Stage | Feeling | The question underneath |
|-------|---------|-------------------------|
| 1. Nomad | Defensive | "Why now, and why for someone like me?" |
| 2. Observer | Curious but stuck | "Is it safe to start?" |
| 3. Dabbler | Excited but inconsistent | "Is the investment worth what I'll get back?" |
| 4. Integrator | Quietly confident | "What could I build with this — for other people?" |
| 5. Architect | Restless, hesitant | "Do I actually believe I'm the kind of person who does this?" |
| 6. Innovator | Restless, building | "What's broken in my field that nobody fixes?" |

---

## Stage 1 — Nomad

*Feeling: defensive.*

Nomads aren't ignorant. They've seen the headlines, sat through the meetings, heard the enthusiasm. They've made a choice — consciously or not — not to engage. Often it's principled: scepticism about accuracy, concern about what AI is doing to work, a pattern of watching hype cycles come and go. The question underneath isn't "how does this work?" It's "why now, and why for someone like me?"

What moves a Nomad isn't more evidence that AI is real. It's a reason that connects to something they already care about.

## Stage 2 — Observer

*Feeling: curious but stuck.*

Observers have decided AI is probably worth paying attention to. They read the articles. They watch what colleagues do. They may have created an account somewhere. But they haven't really started. What holds them isn't a lack of interest — it's a lack of permission. Permission to try something messy, to not know what they're doing. The question: "Is it safe to start?"

More information doesn't move them. A low-stakes first attempt does.

## Stage 3 — Dabbler

*Feeling: excited but inconsistent.*

Dabblers have tried it and had at least one moment of genuine surprise — *that was actually good.* But the habit hasn't formed. They use AI when they remember, when the task obviously fits. The problem is inconsistency: sessions that work brilliantly alternate with sessions that waste half an hour. Until the wins reliably outweigh the friction, the Dabbler is asking: "Is the investment worth what I'll get back?"

What moves them is a repeatable win — one or two tasks where AI works every time.

## Stage 4 — Integrator

*Feeling: quietly confident.*

Integrators have made AI a genuine part of how they work. Not a novelty — a habit. They save time consistently, know what the tools handle well, and have stopped needing to convince themselves. The scepticism is gone; the excitement has become practical competence.

But most Integrators are optimising for themselves — their own output, their own tasks. That's real, and it's where most people stop. The question that would move them is one they haven't asked yet: "What could I build with this — for other people?"

## Stage 5 — Architect

*Feeling: restless, but hesitant.*

This is the stage most frameworks skip entirely, and it's where capable people stall longest.

An Architect is sketching. They can see the shape of something they'd like to build — a process that would help their team, a tool that would solve a problem they understand better than anyone, a framework they've carried in their head for years. They're starting to think about what other people need, not just what makes their own work easier. The capability is there. The vision is forming.

They just haven't committed to being someone who builds.

The Architect sits between Integrator and Innovator, and they stay there longer than at any other stage. Not because the jump is technically steep — because it asks a question they've been quietly avoiding: "Do I actually believe I'm the kind of person who does this?"

This is what I've watched stop capable professionals more reliably than any skill gap. The people who stall here aren't the ones who need more training. They've done the training, built the competence, earned the confidence in their own tool use — and are still waiting for something. Permission, maybe. Or proof that the next step belongs to them.

## Stage 6 — Innovator

*Feeling: restless, building.*

Innovators build outward — tools, content, services, systems that didn't exist before they made them. Their work has stopped being about getting their own job done faster and become about what they can put into the world. The question has shifted entirely: "What's broken in my field that nobody fixes?"

Most Innovators describe a specific restlessness. Once you can see what's possible, it's hard to go back to just getting through the week.

---

## The gap that matters

The biggest gap in this model isn't between Stage 1 and Stage 6. It's between Stage 4 and Stage 5 — the Integrator who has proven they can use AI well, but hasn't taken the step toward building for others.

That gap has a specific shape, and it has almost nothing to do with skills. It's a **self-permission gap**: the distance between what you know and who you believe does this kind of thing. Those are different problems, and nearly every training programme addresses only the first.

## What actually moves people

If the barrier is identity rather than skill, the usual interventions miss. More training doesn't touch it. Neither does better onboarding or a company-wide strategy. Those address what someone knows, not what they believe about themselves.

Three things tend to move people, and none of them scale like an org chart:

- **Proximity to someone one stage ahead** — not a keynote speaker, a peer doing a version of what you'd like to do who isn't particularly extraordinary. It quietly breaks the assumption that the next step belongs to a different type of person.
- **The right question at the right moment** — not "have you tried this tool?" but "whose problem do you keep noticing that nobody's solving?" It surfaces the thing they've been carrying without examining.
- **Specific permission from someone credible** — not generic encouragement, but recognition from someone who's been where they are and can say, without hedging, *you have what this takes.*

The belief shifts in relationship, not in isolation.
